Stop reading for a second and do this. It takes under a minute and I promise it is worth it.

1. Open Google Maps on your phone.
2. Search your own business name.
3. Tap the **Website** button on your listing.

What loaded?

If the answer is "my website, and it looks fine" — good, genuinely. Skip to the last section, because there is still something worth knowing. If the answer is anything else, keep reading, because you are in very good company and it is fixable.

## What "broken" actually looks like

When I check local listings around Queens, the broken ones fall into a handful of buckets. See if any of these look familiar.

**The parked domain.** A page that says something like "This domain is for sale" or shows a generic template with a hosting company's logo. Your registration lapsed, somebody scooped it, and now your Google listing sends every customer to a page trying to sell your own name back to you.

**The expired site.** The domain still works but the hosting stopped. You get an error, a blank page, or a wall of text about a database connection.

**The spam redirect.** This is the ugly one. The domain got resold and now points at whatever the new owner wants — junk listings, a sketchy shopping page, sometimes gambling or adult content. Your business name is on Google Maps, and the link under it goes somewhere you would be mortified to be associated with. Owners are usually horrified to learn this. They had no idea.

**The site that never worked on a phone.** It loads, technically. Then it demands a pinch-zoom, the menu is a photo of a PDF, the phone number is not tappable, and the hours are from three renovations ago. Roughly everyone finding you is on a phone.

**The "under construction" page.** Somebody started a redesign in 2021. It is still under construction.

**The wrong business entirely.** Rare, but it happens — a listing gets merged or edited and the website field ends up pointing at a different company.

## How this happens to good businesses

Nobody in this situation was careless. The pattern is almost always the same.

Somebody sold you a website years ago. Maybe a relative's kid, maybe a guy who came around the neighborhood, maybe an agency that seemed fine at the time. They built it, took the payment, and registered the domain and the hosting **in their own name** — which is the part that causes all the damage later.

Then they moved on. The renewal notices went to their inbox, or to an email address of yours nobody has opened in six years. The card on file expired. The hosting lapsed. The domain dropped, got picked up by someone who buys expired domains in bulk, and started pointing wherever they wanted.

Meanwhile, you are behind the counter running the business. You do not click your own website link. Why would you? You already know where your store is.

So the break can sit there for *years* while every person who looks you up walks into it.

## Why a dead link costs more than you would think

It is easy to think of the website as decoration — nice to have, not where the money comes from. Here is why it is more than that now.

**Customers who bounce do not come back.** Somebody looks you up, taps your link, lands on a "domain for sale" page, and quietly concludes you are closed. They do not call to check. They tap back and pick the next place on the list. You never hear about it and it never shows up in any number you look at.

**Search engines read it as a signal.** Google looks at whether the information about your business is consistent and whether the site it points to is real and working. A dead link does not just fail to help you — it actively works against your listing's credibility.

**AI assistants need something to cite.** This is the newer piece, and it is the one that will matter most over the next few years. When someone asks ChatGPT or Gemini "best hardware store near Woodside," the model builds an answer out of sources it can point at. It wants a real page — one with your services, your hours, your area, in text it can read.

No working page means nothing to cite. And a model that has nothing to cite about you does the natural thing: it names the shop that *does* have a working page. You do not get an error message. You just get left out, silently, of every conversation.

That is the whole game now. Being findable is not enough. You have to be **citable**.

## The five-minute self-check

Run all of these. Write down what you find.

**1. The Maps link.** The one from the top of this article. Tap it on your phone, not just your laptop.

**2. Search your own name.** Type your business name plus your neighborhood into Google — "Sunrise Hardware Astoria." Does the right listing come up? Are the hours correct? The phone number? Is there a second, duplicate listing for your business that you have never claimed?

**3. Check your hours everywhere.** Google, Yelp, your website, your door. If your website says one thing and your listing says another, both lose credibility.

**4. Search like a customer, not like the owner.** Do not search your business name. Search what someone who does not know you would type: "hardware store open now near me," "best wings in Jackson Heights," "24 hour locksmith Elmhurst." Do you come up at all? Who does?

**5. Ask an AI assistant.** Open ChatGPT or Gemini and ask the way a real person would: *"I'm in Ridgewood, where should I go for authentic tacos?"* See if you are named. See who is. Do it a few times — the answers vary, which is exactly why one lucky screenshot does not tell you much.

That last step is the one almost nobody does, and it is where the surprises are.

## What to do about it

**If your domain lapsed:** find out who registered it. If it is still available, register it yourself, in your own name, with your own email and your own card. Set it to auto-renew. This is the single most important thing you own online and it should never sit in somebody else's account.

**If your domain was resold and points at junk:** you may not be able to get it back cheaply. Do not panic. The faster fix is to get a real, working page up on a new domain and update the website field on your Google listing to point at it. A simple page with your name, address, phone, hours, what you sell, and the areas you serve beats a broken link enormously.

**If your site works but nobody can read it on a phone:** fix the basics before anything fancy. Tappable phone number, readable hours in actual text (not inside an image), your address, your neighborhood, and what you actually sell in plain sentences. Search engines and AI models both read text. They cannot read your menu if it is a photo.

**Whatever you do, claim your Google Business Profile.** Free, and it is the record everything else is built on.

## One honest expectation

Fixing a broken link does not fix your visibility on Monday.

Search engines and AI models re-crawl on their own schedule and are slow to change their minds about a business. **Give it around 45 days** before the change shows up in what they say about you. Anybody promising faster is selling something.

But 45 days from a fix is a lot better than another two years of sending every customer who looks you up to a page that sells your own name back to you.
