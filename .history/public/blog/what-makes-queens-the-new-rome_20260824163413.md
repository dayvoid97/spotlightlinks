# Your City Is Rome: The 13 Pillars Holding Up Every Local Economy

Long before it was an empire, Rome was a city-state — a handful of tribes on seven hills, trading grain, tools, and trust with their neighbors. It grew not because it was destined to, but because the people inside it built something worth defending, then worth expanding.

Every city we've studied follows the same pattern. Long before anyone calls it a "market," it's just neighbors relying on each other for the things that don't change: someone to watch the kids, someone to fix a tax return, someone to teach a kid self-defense, someone to pull an espresso shot at 7 a.m.

At Spotlight Links, we spent time walking those categories in our own backyard — Queens, New York, arguably the most business-dense, most immigrant-founded square footage of small enterprise in the country. What we found wasn't a marketing framework. It was a pattern already there. We just wrote it down.

## The Privately Owned Pillars of Society

This is New York. A big shout out to the public sector helping New Yorkers everyday.
This isn't an exhaustive census — we didn't count hardware stores, hospitals, or funeral homes, and dozens of other categories deserve their own research. But across four broad groups, the same 13 trades kept showing up as the backbone of daily life:

**Private Institutions** — Childcare, adult care, daycare, Montessori schools, education, and technology providers.

**Where People Gather** — Cafes, restaurants, bars, convenience stores, and laundromats.

**Professional Tangible Services** — Plumbing & electrical, HVAC, hardware & home improvement, auto repair.

**Professional Intangible Services** — Tax and accounting, legal services, and life, property, and business insurance.

**Human Wellbeing** — Self-defense and martial arts, fitness and equipment training, skill acquisition, and driving schools.

Every one of these is a trade a resident turns to regularly, not occasionally. And in a place like Queens, most of these categories have 30 or more active operators fighting for the same customers, often within a few subway stops of each other.

That's the part that doesn't get talked about enough: **competition inside a category is fierce, but visibility across the category is still wide open.**
